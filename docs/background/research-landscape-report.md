# Scientific research stuck between disconnected tools

Scientific research does not follow a straight line. It is a chain of loops blocked by approval "gates" (funding, research ethics, committees, peer review), and most of the pain felt by both scientists and students appears where these loops and gates meet disconnected tools, and where documents are hard to find, hard to trust and hard to keep. The sources show three notable points. First, there is no single "standard" process model; frameworks such as the data lifecycle, preregistration, CRISP-DM or field-specific empirical standards each cover only one slice, and many of the most time-consuming activities are loops (data cleaning, peer review, revision). Second, the tool landscape is fragmented and concentrated in a few corporations: **80%** of researchers use general-purpose chatbots but only **11%** know of specialized research tools (a survey funded by a publisher), while no independent audit confirms the accuracy of most AI literature-search tools. Third, the pains that are best measured are workload, supervision/mentoring, career instability, mental health and reproducibility; roughly **one third to more than two fifths** of doctoral students show moderate to severe anxiety or depression symptoms, but almost all large surveys are self-selected and conducted in English. The most prominent needs are time, intermediate-level training, affordable and predictable storage, AI tools with verifiable citations, and automated compliance (data management plans, open access). On literature, the strongest evidence shows "information exists but is hard to trust and hard to keep": the number of papers grows by about **4% per year**, retractions exceeded **10,000** in 2023, and the statement "data available upon request" is nearly useless in practice. Evidence for Vietnam is very thin and mostly small studies; the report states clearly which parts are facts and which are inferences.

## Research process: ten stages, many loops, three types of gates

The sources do not give a single standard process but several frameworks that complement each other. The DCC Curation Lifecycle Model (Higgins, 2008) describes managing data "from the moment an idea is formed" through an iterative cycle, stressing that the information most important for reuse must be recorded at the stage when the idea is formed and data is collected ([DCC](https://www.dcc.ac.uk/faq/dcc-curation-lifecycle-model)). Munafo et al. (2017) propose a "reproducible science" model with five groups of measures: methods, reporting and dissemination, reproducibility, evaluation, and incentives ([Munafo et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7610724/)). For data/ML projects, CRISP-DM has six phases (business understanding, data understanding, data preparation, modeling, evaluation, deployment) and is described as a cycle rather than a rigid sequence; the data preparation phase often repeats many times ([GWU summary](https://tech.revu.gwu.edu/post/intro-to-data-science-understanding-crisp-dm)). Some fields have their own standards: the ACM SIGSOFT Empirical Standards for software engineering research include **19 method-specific standards**, with checklists for authors and reviewers, and are recommended for guiding graduate students as well ([SIGSOFT](https://www2.sigsoft.org/EmpiricalStandards/)); the NeurIPS conference requires a paper checklist (missing it leads to immediate rejection) covering code, data, error bars, computing resources and ethics approval ([NeurIPS](https://neurips.cc/public/guides/PaperChecklist)).

From these scattered frameworks, the research note proposes a synthetic ten-stage lifecycle. This is **the note author's inference, not a conclusion of the sources**: (1) idea, question and gap analysis; (2) literature review; (3) hypotheses, design and analysis plan, optionally preregistration or Stage 1 Registered Report; (4) funding application and ethics approval; (5) data collection/experiments with careful records; (6) analysis, separating confirmatory and exploratory analysis; (7) writing the manuscript and posting a preprint; (8) peer review and revision; (9) publication, archiving data and code under FAIR principles; (10) post-publication: corrections, reproduction, reuse. Each stage produces its own intermediate artifacts: grant proposal with a data management plan and ethics documents; protocol or preregistration; lab notebooks, raw data, version-controlled code repositories; scripts and analysis logs; manuscript and preprint; review letters and response letters; the published article with data/code bearing a DOI. A preregistration is a time-stamped registry entry recording the hypotheses, statistical tests, analysis plan and variable definitions; analyses outside the plan are still performed but must be labeled exploratory ([COS Prereg](https://www.cos.io/initiatives/prereg)). Under ORI guidance, lab notebooks must be sufficient for outsiders to follow without verbal explanation, including negative results, and are commonly kept for at least **5 years** after the research ends (a common institutional benchmark, not a universal rule) ([ORI](https://ori.hhs.gov/education/products/wsu/data_lab.html)).

Loops appear at almost every stage. In CRISP-DM, a better understanding of the data leads back to refining the business problem; a failed evaluation leads back to the early phases ([GWU](https://tech.revu.gwu.edu/post/intro-to-data-science-understanding-crisp-dm)). Grounded theory qualitative research collects and analyzes data simultaneously in a zig-zag pattern and stops at "theoretical saturation"; collecting all interviews before analyzing them eliminates the loop mechanism ([Lingard et al., BMJ 2008](https://depts.washington.edu/fammed/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/lingard_2008_bmj_-_grounded_theory_mixed_methods_and_action_research.pdf)). The most costly peer-review loop is in latency: a pilot survey of 337 clinical research authors (21% response rate, with recall bias) found a median of **5 months** until acceptance and **7 months** until publication ([Wallach et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803634/)); an analysis of general medical journals reported a median of 224 days from submission to publication ([Scientometrics 2019](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11192-019-03061-8)). Registered Reports reverse the main gate order: peer review happens before data collection, the Stage 1 decision averages about **9 weeks** excluding author revision time, and "in-principle" acceptance is not revoked just because results turn out negative ([COS Registered Reports](https://www.cos.io/initiatives/registered-reports)). In other words, the largest structural improvement in open science is "moving the gates forward" (inference from the notes).

There are three types of gates. Ethics gates: under the US Common Rule, human-subject research that is not exempt must be approved by an IRB before it begins; only the full board has the authority to reject it ([Univ. of Iowa HSO](https://hso.research.uiowa.edu/node/961)). Funding gates: NIH review cycles run about 4–5 months after the submission deadline, and the council about 7 months after the deadline ([NIAID](https://www.niaid.nih.gov/grants-contracts/overview-r01-process)); from 25 January 2023, applications that generate scientific data must include a data management and sharing plan ([NIDA](https://nida.nih.gov/research/nih-policies-guidance/guidance-nih-data-management-sharing-policy)). Publication gates: NeurIPS 2025 received **21,575** valid submissions and accepted **5,290** (24.52%) ([NeurIPS blog](https://blog.neurips.cc/2025/09/30/reflections-on-the-2025-review-process-from-the-program-committee-chairs/)). The R01 success rate figure (13.0% for fiscal year 2025) comes only from a blog, of low reliability, and is not used as a fact.

For students, the gates have different names but a similar structure. The typical US path is coursework, qualifying exams, forming a committee, defending a proposal, becoming a candidate, research and writing, and a final defense. Illinois CS has an average time of about 6 years and a hard limit of 7 years ([Illinois CS](https://siebelschool.illinois.edu/academics/graduate/phd-program/phd-time-limits-milestones)), while Indiana Sociology usually takes 7–8 years ([Indiana](https://sociology.indiana.edu/student-portal/graduate/phd-degree/phd-timeline/index.html)). Time to degree differs by field: a median of **6.7 years** in physical sciences and engineering versus **12.7 years** in education (2007–08 data, now outdated) ([CGS](https://legacy.cgsnet.org/ckfinder/userfiles/files/DataSources_2010_03.pdf)). The limits must be stated clearly: the sources have no timelines for undergraduate theses, master's programs, or doctoral models in the UK, Europe or Vietnam; the difference between experts and students in the process (experts add grant writing, team management, review and supervision; students follow program milestones and their supervisor's schedule) is an inference without a direct source.

The overall picture has three features. The first is that users choose free, general-purpose tools: the Wiley ExplanAItions survey of August 2025 (n=2,430, **funded by the publisher**) found that **80%** use common tools such as ChatGPT compared with **25%** using AI research assistants, **70%** rely on free tools, and only **11%** know of the specialized tools covered in the survey ([Wiley](https://newsroom.wiley.com/press-releases/press-release-details/2025/AI-Adoption-Jumps-to-84-Among-Researchers-as-Expectations-Undergo-Significant-Reality-Check/default.aspx)). Price and ease of use, rather than specialized features, seem to determine adoption (inference from the notes). The second is that the reliability of the AI layer has not been independently verified. Specialized tools advertise real citations, but the only independent audit in the notes (about Scite) gave unfavorable results: Scite classified 2 citations as "supporting" and 96 as "merely mentioning", while the evaluators found 42 supporting, 39 mentioning and 17 contradicting; no independent audit was found for Consensus, Elicit or SciSpace ([IntuitionLabs](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/citation-reliability-ai-literature-reviews), aggregated source). For general-purpose language models, the fabricated-citation rate ranged from 18% (GPT-4, Walters and Wilder 2023) to 55% (GPT-3.5) in the same study ([Sci Rep 2023 via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/fc88ea07ec994a7c8a144f8516c48cbf)), and 91.4% for Bard in the study by Chelli et al. ([JMIR 2024](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e53164)); an independent review of Sakana's "AI Scientist" found structural errors and fabricated data in the generated manuscripts ([arXiv 2502.14297](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14297)). The note concluded that "a real link" does not mean "a link that supports the claim", so every AI citation must still be checked. The third feature is non-transparent concentration of ownership and commercialization. The same corporation owns the bibliographic database, the research management system and sometimes the journals (Elsevier with Scopus, Pure, Mendeley; Clarivate with Web of Science, Converis), so data flows between stages tend to favor the company's ecosystem; this is an inference from ownership facts. Prices for electronic lab notebooks and research management systems are mostly not public. Recent acquisitions include Litmaps buying ResearchRabbit (May 2025, according to trade press) ([IT Brief NZ](https://itbrief.co.nz/story/litmaps-acquires-researchrabbit-raises-1-million-for-ai)), and the CoreWeave acquisition of W&B mentioned above increases vendor dependency risk, which is an inference; no source records user complaints. Gaps must be stated clearly: prices for Consensus, Scite, SciSpace, Connected Papers, Litmaps and ResearchRabbit come only from aggregators; Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, Notion, Obsidian, Jupyter, MLflow, DVC and other writing tools have no verified sources; no survey directly measures the friction of moving data between tools.

## Pains of scientists and students: supervision, instability, burnout and non-reproducible experiments

Evidence strength convention in the notes: strong is peer-reviewed research or large-scale empirical analysis; medium is large self-selected surveys by journals, funders or associations; weak is surveys funded by publishers or vendors, blogs, small local studies, or figures seen only through secondary summaries.

On doctoral students' mental health, two independent studies are highly consistent. Levecque et al. (2017), a representative sample of **3,659** doctoral students in Flanders, found **32%** at risk of common psychiatric disorders, linked to the supervisor's leadership style, group culture and career prospects outside academia ([UKCGE](https://supervision.ukcge.ac.uk/resources/resource-library/work-organization-and-mental-health-problems-in-phd-students)); this is the strongest evidence available, but the notes could not extract an effect size. Evans et al. (2018, Nature Biotechnology), a convenience sample of **2,279** people from 26 countries, recorded **41%** with moderate to severe anxiety and **39%** with depression, compared with about 6% in the general population according to press summaries; the authors themselves noted that a convenience sample may inflate rates ([Chemistry World](https://chemistryworld.com/3008862.article)). Nature's 2019 doctoral survey (more than 6,000 people, self-selected, skewed toward natural sciences, data via secondary summaries) found about **21%** had been bullied (mainly by their supervisor), **36%** had sought help for anxiety or depression caused by their program, and more than three quarters worked over 40 hours per week ([Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/one-three-phd-students-seeks-mental-health-help-says-survey)). The cautious citation, drawn by the note author from converging sources, is "about one third or more", not a population rate. Supervisors appear in every dataset as the most distinguishing factor for well-being and satisfaction; however, the Advance HE PRES 2024 survey (more than 12,000 postgraduate researchers in the UK and Australia, **81%** satisfied) shows that satisfaction measures paint a brighter picture than stress scales, partly because of survivorship bias ([Advance HE](https://advance-he.ac.uk/news-and-views/postgraduate-researchers-report-high-satisfaction-despite-cost-living-pressures/)). Figures on supervisor meeting time also need caution: about half of doctoral students spend under 1 hour per week according to Nature (via secondary data), 63% in the UK according to HEPI quotations, and the Nature 2025 figures are seen only through a summary.

For researchers, the Wellcome 2020 research culture survey (n=4,267, self-selected, 76% in the UK) found **84%** proud to do research but only **29%** feeling secure pursuing a career; **43%** had been bullied or harassed, 78–80% thought intense competition creates an unfriendly environment, and only **14%** thought metrics had a positive impact ([Wellcome](https://wellcome.org/press-release/largest-survey-research-culture-reveals-high-levels-stress-and-insecurity)). Elsevier's "Researcher of the Future" 2025 survey (more than 3,200 people, 113 countries, **funded by the publisher**) found only **45%** had enough time for research, **68%** felt publication pressure had increased compared with 2–3 years earlier, and only **33%** expected funding in their field to increase ([Elsevier](https://www.elsevier.com/about/press-releases/elseviers-global-survey-of-3-000-researchers-reveals-less-than-half-have)). The administrative burden is best measured in the US: Federal Demonstration Partnership workload surveys (about 13,000 respondents in 2012) found about **42%** of principal investigators' research time went to administrative requirements ([Chemistry World](https://chemistryworld.com/news/almost-half-of-us-researchers-time-goes-on-admin/7728.article)); the 44.3% figure for the 2018 survey has not been verified. This figure refers only to principal investigators in the US and does not represent students or other countries.

Reproducibility is the pain with the strongest empirical evidence in computational work. An analysis of about 1.4 million Jupyter notebooks on GitHub found only about **24%** ran without errors and only about **4%** produced the same results, with common causes being missing dependencies, hidden state and execution order, and inaccessible data ([Pimentel et al., MSR 2019](https://2019.msrconf.org/details/msr-2019-papers/36/A-Large-scale-Study-about-Quality-and-Reproducibility-of-Jupyter-Notebooks)). Data leakage was found in 22 papers across 17 fields, affecting at least 294 papers; in civil war prediction, the advantage of complex models disappeared after fixing the leakage ([Kapoor and Narayanan, Patterns 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10499856)). At the general experimental level, Baker's survey (Nature 2016, about 1,500 self-selected people) found that more than **70%** had failed to reproduce another researcher's experiment and more than half had failed to reproduce their own, but this is self-reported experience, not the actual rate of non-reproducible results ([FORRT](https://www.forrt.org/curated_resources/2_1-500-scientists-lift-the-lid-on-reprodu/)). The same survey showed that only 33% said their institution had taken concrete steps to improve. Publication pressure is an amplifying factor throughout: it appears as a cause of non-reproducibility (Baker), the top concern of students (Nature 2025) and a cultural feature (Wellcome); this part is the note author's synthesis.

For personal data management, the evidence is much weaker: a survey of 360 Canadian graduate students (a blog post) found 14.2% had lost data and had to recollect it ([UMich Library](https://blogs.lib.umich.edu/bits-and-pieces/love-your-data-week-keep-your-data-safe)); other surveys had only 75–80 respondents. There is no reliable large survey on tool fragmentation, literature overload, lack of statistical skills as a separate pain, delays in ethics approval, or breakdown of collaboration. For Vietnam, there are only small studies in Vietnamese: a survey of master's students in Ho Chi Minh City identified work pressure, lack of funding and family obligations as the most widely agreed barriers ([NTT Journal](https://jst.ntt.edu.vn/index.php/jst/article/download/464/450/1803)); Vietnamese doctoral students mostly pay their own tuition and face international publication requirements (press commentary, weak evidence) ([Đại biểu Nhân dân](https://daibieunhandan.vn/5-yeu-to-nang-cao-chat-luong-dao-tao-tien-si-10286298.html)). For undergraduates, recurring themes are time management, topic selection, writing skills, methodology and access to supervisors, but the sources are small qualitative studies mostly from Indonesia and EFL contexts; transferability to Vietnam is unverified. No nationally representative survey of Vietnamese doctoral students or researchers exists.

## Needs: time, intermediate training, verifiable AI and automatic compliance

Needs are most clearly expressed in the Ithaka S+R 2021 report (213 interviews at 21–23 institutions; **not a representative sample**, and computing accounted for only 12%). Cleaning and processing data is the most labor-intensive stage and is often given to junior members; one public health professor estimated it takes **80%** of a project's time. Data is scattered (GitHub, databases, lab websites), institutional repositories often lack capacity for lab-scale data, many people keep data on local devices and avoid cloud storage because recurring fees are hard to justify in grant budgets. For training, researchers prefer self-directed learning through online guides; library workshops are seen as too basic, leaving a gap for intermediate users; doctoral students often lack statistical, data processing and command-line foundations; version control, file naming and metadata are frequent friction points in collaboration ([Ithaka S+R](https://sr.ithaka.org/publications/big-data-infrastructure-at-the-crossroads/)). Elsevier's 2025 survey adds the same message from the time perspective: only 45% have enough research time, a fact from a publisher-funded survey.

On AI, usage is already the majority behavior but trust is low. Wiley: AI use rose from 57% to **84%** (2024–2025), but concern about errors/hallucinations rose from 51% to **64%** and privacy concerns from 47% to 58%; **57%** cited lack of guidance/training as a barrier and **73%** want publishers to give clear guidance. Elsevier: **58%** use AI (37% in 2024), with the top uses being finding and summarizing literature (61%) and literature reviews (51%); only 27% feel adequately trained and only 22% consider AI tools trustworthy; factors that increase trust are automatic reference citation (59%), training on peer-reviewed content (55%) and periodic expert checks (49%). Both surveys were funded by publishers, so the model "users want AI tied to publisher content" should be read with caution about commercial motives. A Springer Nature survey published in July 2026 (10,480 responses, 117 countries, also publisher-funded) found **75.9%** of mentioned tools were general-purpose models and only **11%** said their institution funded their main tool ([Springer Nature](https://group.springernature.com/br/group/media/press-releases/early-career-researchers-lead-shift-towards-ai-first-discovery/52988132)). For doctoral students, the Nature 2025 survey (3,785 people, 107 countries, self-selected, data via secondary summary) shows positive attitudes toward AI for monitoring and summarizing literature, and negative attitudes toward AI for writing papers, analyzing data and designing experiments; a non-peer-reviewed preprint analyzing this survey has an unusual arXiv identifier, so it is not used as a primary fact. For UK undergraduates, HEPI/Kortext (n=1,041, co-funded by a vendor) found **92%** use AI but only **36%** had been trained by their university ([HEPI](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2025/)). Publishing policies converge on AI not being listed as an author, significant use having to be disclosed, and stricter rules for images and peer-review confidentiality: COPE states that AI cannot take responsibility or manage copyright ([COPE](https://publicationethics.org/node/56046)); Elsevier requires a separate AI declaration and prohibits reviewers from uploading manuscripts to AI tools ([Elsevier](https://www.elsevier.com/about/policies-and-standards/generative-ai-policies-for-journals)).

On compliance, funder and journal requirements are the strongest drivers of formal data sharing. NIH has required data management and sharing plans since 25 January 2023; Horizon Europe requires immediate open access under CC BY and FAIR data in trusted repositories ([Masaryk Univ.](https://openscience.muni.cz/en/services/project-consulting/open-science-requirements-in-projects/horizon-europe)); UKRI has required open access for articles from 1 April 2022 and for books/chapters from 1 January 2024 ([UKRI](https://www.ukri.org/publications/ukri-open-access-policy/)). The "State of Open Data" 2024 report (**conducted by Springer Nature, Digital Science and Figshare**, sample size not stated on the page) says **69%** cited a lack of recognition for sharing data, and awareness of FAIR rose from 15% (2018) to 40% (2025), but the year-to-year figures are not consistent ([Springer Nature](https://springernature.com/gp/researchers/campaigns/state-of-open-data)). Another need is less measured: research software engineers (RSEs) prioritize formal recognition of their role and career paths ([SSI](https://www.software.ac.uk/blog/what-do-we-know-about-rses-results-our-international-surveys), detailed results not obtained). No survey directly measures the need to trace from a claim to data and experiments, the use of electronic lab notebooks, or the needs of undergraduates, lab heads and research administrators outside FDP/SRA, so these needs can only be inferred from the pains.

## Research literature: more, more open, but less trustworthy and easier to lose

**Overload and growth.** Bornmann, Haunschild and Mutz (2021), combining Dimensions, Microsoft Academic, Web of Science and Scopus, estimate growth of about **4.1% per year**, meaning the literature doubles roughly every **17.3 years** ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/9b8a18f3a3124b25a0f47e766b0f7ca6)); STM 2022 reports 5–6.5% per year for 2015–2020 via a secondary summary ([STM](https://stm-assoc.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/2022_08_24_STM_White_Report_a4_v15.pdf)). Preprints grew from about 3,000 (1991) to about 227,000 (2019) ([arXiv 2102.09066](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.09066)); Crossref had about 156 million records (April 2024) and about 180 million (March 2026) ([Crossref](https://crossref.org/blog/2026-public-data-file-now-available/)). The inferred consequence: the volume of literature a doctoral student must survey can double within one doctoral career; there is no survey data on the feeling of "not keeping up", and no verified total of papers per year for 2023–2025.

**Access.** Open access is growing but gaps remain. Piwowar et al. (2018) estimated at least **28%** of the literature is open access ([Infodocket](https://www.infodocket.com/2018/02/14/research-article-the-state-of-oa-a-large-scale-analysis-of-the-prevalence-and-impact-of-open-access-articles-final-version/)); an OpenAlex analysis reported via a secondary summary shows growth from 22% (2015) to a peak of 59% (2023); this is unverified and cannot be directly compared because the methods differ ([Rogue Scholar](https://rogue-scholar.org/records/1t1dg-xha78)). Sci-Hub contained 85.1% of paywalled journal articles as of March 2017 ([Himmelstein et al., eLife 2018](https://elifesciences.org/articles/32822v1)). A small global survey (N=376, 76 countries, via summary) found **48%** of researchers in the Global South often encounter blocked articles, compared with 27% in the Global North ([arXiv 2509.08299](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.08299)). The barrier is shifting from reading fees to publishing fees (APCs): estimated 2023 APC revenue was USD 681.6 million (MDPI), 582.8 million (Elsevier), 546.6 million (Springer Nature), calculated from list prices, not audited ([Haustein et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16551v1)). In Vietnam, a survey of 238 lecturers from 34 institutions (May–July 2024) found that schools and the Ministry of Education contribute little to awareness of open access ([report](https://oesr.thanhdo.edu.vn/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/42.-REK-Vietnamese-researchers-knowledge-attitudes-and-practices-regarding-open-access-publishing-a-cross-sectional-study.pdf), only an excerpt was seen); there are no Vietnamese figures on access to paywalled journals, library budgets or Sci-Hub.

**Quality and trust.** Nature reported more than **10,000** retractions in 2023, over 8,000 of them from Hindawi (Wiley), and a retraction rate that tripled in a decade to above 0.2% ([Nature](https://media.nature.com/original/magazine-assets/d41586-023-03974-8/d41586-023-03974-8.pdf)); the Retraction Watch Database has about **63,000** retractions as of the end of 2025 ([Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/?p=128476)). Retraction counts partly reflect better detection and publisher cleanup, not only increased fraud (inference). Richardson et al. (PNAS 2025) found suspected "paper mill" articles doubling about every **1.5 years**, compared with about 15 years for the entire literature, with data up to 2020 ([Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2025/08/04/fighting-coordinated-publication-fraud-is-like-emptying-an-overflowing-bathtub-with-a-spoon-study-coauthor-says/)). On predatory journals, the only estimate is about 8,000 journals and 420,000 articles in 2014, which is outdated ([Shen and Björk](https://bmcmedicine.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12916-015-0469-2)). On AI-fabricated citations, a GPTZero audit (January 2026) found at least 100 fake citations in roughly 51–53 of 4,841 accepted NeurIPS 2025 papers, which passed at least three reviewers per paper; GPTZero sells a verification product, so it has a conflict of interest ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/01/21/neurips-ai-conferences-research-papers-hallucinations/)). LLM-generated summaries overgeneralized in 26–73% of cases across three models in a Royal Society Open Science 2025 study ([arXiv 2504.00025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.00025)). Not all errors come from AI: the pooled quotation error rate was about **25.4%**, and the 2025 update (46 studies, about 32,000 citations) showed no improvement over time ([Baethge and Jergas 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12285159/)). For Vietnam, only one article from January 2026, about a forum in Ho Chi Minh City, says Vietnam publishes more than 15,000 papers per year and is among the 10 countries with the most retractions, without specific retraction figures ([Tuổi Trẻ](https://news.tuoitre.vn/vietnam-seeks-solutions-as-research-paper-retractions-rank-among-worlds-highest-103260126113531606.htm)).

**Retention and management.** Klein et al. (PLOS ONE 2014), studying 1.84 million STM articles (1997–2012), found that about **one in five** articles suffered reference rot and **seven in ten** articles contained web references ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4277367)); Pew 2024 found 38% of web pages that existed in 2013 were no longer accessible in 2023 ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2024/05/17/when-online-content-disappears/)). For data, a 2023 BMJ review (105 studies, more than 2.1 million articles) found a public data availability statement in **8%** of articles but actual public availability in only **2%**, and public code below 0.5% ([Hamilton et al.](https://www.bmj.com/content/382/bmj-2023-075767)). Among 1,792 articles where authors stated they were "willing to share", **93%** did not respond or refused when asked, and only **6.8%** actually provided the data ([Gabelica et al. 2022](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/statistics/bias/2022-gabelica.pdf)). Old data disappears over time: the probability that data still exists falls by about 17% per year in an ecology sample ([Vines et al., Dryad](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.q3g37)), a single field, so it may not generalize. There are no figures for manuscript version control, loss of lab notebooks, duplication in reference managers, or FAIR compliance rates.

**Language, indexing and systematic reviews.** Amano et al. (PLOS Biology 2023, a survey of 908 environmental scientists) found non-native English speakers needed up to **51%** more time to write papers, with rejection rates roughly 2.5 times higher (measured as a multiplier via secondary press releases) ([THE](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/727123)). In June 2019, among 387 recognized Vietnamese journals, only 12 (3.1%) were in the ASEAN Citation Index and 6 (1.6%) were in Scopus/ESCI ([eScienceEditing](https://escienceediting.org/journal/view.php?number=179)); the inference is that Vietnamese-language literature is nearly invisible to international indexes, but there are no figures on the share of Vietnamese-language papers in Google Scholar or OpenAlex. Systematic reviews take a long time: on average about 67.3 weeks from registration to submission across 195 reviews in PROSPERO ([Borah et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5337708)); one study was completed in only 2 weeks using automated tools with a small experienced team and dedicated time ([Clark et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32004673/)). No time-saving percentages are available for tools such as ASReview, Rayyan or Covidence.

## Strength of evidence and gaps that must be stated plainly

Evidence strength is uneven across the five questions. The strongest is the literature domain (large-scale analyses, peer-reviewed studies) and empirical studies of computational reproducibility. Medium strength applies to large but self-selected surveys by Nature, Wellcome and Baker, and to the Ithaka S+R report, which itself states it is not representative. Weak evidence includes surveys funded by publishers or vendors (Wiley, Elsevier, Springer Nature, Digital Science/Figshare, HEPI/Kortext), price tables from aggregators, figures seen only through secondary summaries (many Nature 2019 and 2025 figures, Baker 2016), and small local studies in Vietnam. Notably, the publisher-funded surveys provide most of the figures on AI and data sharing, so the parties with commercial interests are the ones shaping the picture.

Key gaps are as follows. There are no authoritative sources for the classic "scientific method" steps or methods textbooks (Creswell, Kumar), the full list of DCC steps, the original CRISP-DM 1.0 document, or Nosek 2018 (seen only via summary). There are no timelines for university bachelor's, master's or doctoral models in the UK, Europe or Vietnam; no time data for ethics approval, for funders outside NIH (UKRI, NSF, ERC, NAFOSTED), or for post-publication processes. On tools, there are no independent accuracy audits for Elicit, Consensus or SciSpace; many tools have no verified sources (Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, Notion, Obsidian, Jupyter, MLflow, DVC); there is no survey on acceptance of non-AI tools and no direct evidence measuring integration friction between stages. On pains and needs, there is no large survey on tool fragmentation, literature overload, statistical skills, collaboration breakdown, tracing arguments to data, or the needs of undergraduates and lab heads; there is no ranking of pains by both frequency and severity (the note's way of contrasting "common versus serious" is the note author's synthesis). For Vietnam, there is no nationally representative survey, no rate of mental health problems among Vietnamese doctoral students, and no figures on access to literature, data/code sharing, citations or school AI policies. Some figures in the sources conflict and should be used as ranges rather than points: LLM hallucination rates range from 18% to 91.4% depending on model, task and year; 2023 retraction counts range from more than 10,000 (Nature) to 11,884 (secondary site); open access rates of 28% (2018) and 59% (2023) differ because the methods differ.

## Implications for a unified workspace product (INFERENCE, not conclusions of the sources)

This section is the report author's inference based on the findings above, for a product that combines software, research and AI projects. No source verifies it directly, and it has not been tested with real users.

First, a process with gates and loops suggests that the product's data model should make "stages and gates" the central concept, not a flat list of tasks: each project has stages (the ten stages above are a starting point), gates (approval, defense, review) and intermediate artifacts attached to each stage, with the ability to repeat without losing history. Existing standards (the NeurIPS checklist, SIGSOFT Empirical Standards, data management plans, preregistration) can be included as templates that can be completed in parts, because they encode tacit knowledge and students benefit most. For students and doctoral candidates, program milestones and supervisor schedules are the critical path, so a supervisor view with low-intervention features is needed; this is based on the finding that the supervisor relationship appears in every dataset on well-being, but there is no evidence that a software tool improves that relationship.

Second, the three pains with the strongest empirical evidence all meet at a point where a unified workspace has a natural advantage: computational reproducibility (4% of notebooks produce the same results, data leakage), tracing data and code, and loss of data and literature over time. Tying each result to the version of its code, environment, data, parameters and cited sources, capturing web references when they are cited, and exporting to a repository with a DOI are capabilities that the "software" and "AI" parts of the product can offer naturally, whereas most current research tools do not. Conversely, electronic lab notebooks, reference managers and research management systems are areas already dominated by centralized vendors with high switching costs, so open import and export (no user lock-in) will matter more than complete replacement; this is an inference from ownership facts and from the discontinuation of Mendeley Desktop.

Third, the AI layer should be designed around verifiability rather than generation. The evidence shows that citations can be fabricated or misattributed, that summaries and generalizations can be overstated, that all groups use free general-purpose tools, and that the trust factors users mention are automatic citation, peer-reviewed sources and human checks. The implication: every AI output should come with an open reference to the source passage, automatic DOI/title checks, a prompt log and output usable for disclosure in the Methods section, and the option of local models for sensitive data (privacy concern is 58% according to Wiley, a vendor source). Since adoption favors free tools, a low entry price or student plan will matter; the USD 49/month for Elicit Pro is a reference price for the professional tier, and whether students are willing to pay that amount has no data yet.

Fourth, for the Vietnamese market, the evidence is not sufficient to draw conclusions. What can be said is that the themes (funding, workload, supervisors, publication pressure) match the global picture, with two potential distinctive features: students pay their own tuition, and international publication requirements are tied to graduation and academic titles, together with Vietnamese-language literature being almost absent from international indexes. Vietnamese-language support for search and citation, and importing domestic indexes such as ACI or V-CitationGate, are untested opportunities. Before committing, small surveys and interviews with Vietnamese students and lecturers should be conducted to test these assumptions, especially regarding the degree of tool fragmentation and tracing needs, the two areas where the sources provide no data at all.

## Conclusion

Placing the five areas side by side reveals that the best-measured pains lie not in tools but in people and structures (supervisors, career instability, publication pressure), while the pains that software could solve (non-reproducibility, data loss, citation errors, rotting references) are measured through empirical analysis rather than user surveys. In other words, the need with strong evidence has not yet been stated by users as product requirements; conversely, what users say in surveys (they want AI with citations, they want training, they want enough time) largely comes from publishers with commercial interests.

The most important change in how the process is understood is moving decision gates earlier, before data collection, and standardizing record-keeping from the start, because all the evidence on data loss and non-reproducibility points the same way: records written late almost never save the work. The next step is not to collect more large English-language surveys, but to test the assumptions about tool fragmentation, tracing and the Vietnamese context through targeted small studies, because those are exactly the points where the available evidence is completely silent.
